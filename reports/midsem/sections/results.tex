\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All scores are on the held-out sequence of each leave-one-sequence-out fold
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). The primary target is \texttt{err\_mag\_m}.

Tables~\ref{tab:mae} and~\ref{tab:r2} give the headline result. On the two
\emph{easy} sequences every model beats the mean baseline. For the regularised
linear models (Ridge, Lasso, mini-batch GD) and Random Forest, MAE falls by
18--27\% (e.g.\ Ridge on MH\_02: $0.0101 \to 0.0074$\,m), and the models
explain 31--48\% of the test sequence's label variance; unregularised OLS gains
less (Section~\ref{sec:overfitting}). On \emph{MH\_03\_medium} no model beats
the baseline: the tuned models reach MAE $0.0147$--$0.0151$\,m against the
baseline's $0.0145$\,m, with $R^2$ slightly negative, and OLS is clearly worse
($0.0175$\,m, $R^2 = -0.70$). Averaged over folds, Lasso ($0.0099$\,m), Ridge and
mini-batch GD ($0.0100$\,m) and Random Forest ($0.0102$\,m) are within
$0.0003$\,m of each other. With only three test flights that difference is
within noise, so we treat these four models as tied rather than naming a single
best one; the inner-CV scores, which never see the test flight, tell the same
story. A regularised linear model does as well as the non-linear forest on
this data.
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m.csv (column rmse, "mean" rows)
RMSE tells the same story (fold means: baseline $0.0154$, OLS $0.0162$, Ridge
$0.0134$, Lasso $0.0133$, mini-batch GD $0.0134$, Random Forest $0.0136$\,m).

% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_tuning.csv
Tuning chose strong shrinkage for Ridge ($\alpha = 1000$ in every fold, inside
a grid that runs to $10^5$) and $\alpha = 10^{-3}$ for Lasso in every fold,
also inside its grid. For mini-batch GD it chose the L1 penalty in every fold.
For Random Forest it chose shallow or large-leaf trees in two of the three
folds. All these choices point to the same thing: the signal is weak and
over-fitting is the main risk.

\subsection{Over-fitting}
\label{sec:overfitting}

A model over-fits when it learns the noise of its training flights instead of
a relationship that carries over to a new one. The symptom is a small error on
the training windows and a much larger one on the held-out sequence. We
therefore also score every model on the windows it was fitted on
(\texttt{train\_mae}) and compare.

\paragraph{Without a penalty, the linear model over-fits.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m.csv (columns mae, train_mae; "mean" rows)
OLS fits its training flights slightly \emph{better} than Ridge (fold-mean
training MAE $0.0083$ against $0.0086$\,m), but does worse on every held-out
flight (Table~\ref{tab:mae}). Averaged over folds it scores $0.0115$\,m, worse
even than the mean baseline ($0.0113$\,m), while Ridge scores $0.0100$\,m. The
damage is largest on MH\_03\_medium, where OLS reaches $R^2 = -0.70$.
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_tuning.csv (column inner_cv_mae)
The inner cross-validation saw the same thing before any test data was
touched: OLS's inner-CV MAE is $0.045$, $0.028$ and $0.008$\,m in the three
folds, against $0.012$, $0.012$ and $0.007$\,m for Ridge. This is the failure
the L2 penalty is there to prevent (Section~\ref{sec:models}): with many
near-duplicate features, least squares assigns large weights of opposite sign
that cancel on the training flights and not on a new one. Lasso's L1 penalty
goes further and sets all but 8--11 of the 104 weights to exactly zero in each
fold (notebook~05); the features it keeps (\texttt{ax\_mean}/\texttt{ax\_rms},
\texttt{acc\_mag\_mean}, \texttt{az\_spec\_entropy}, \texttt{gy\_mean}) overlap
with those that permutation importance ranks highest below.

\paragraph{Every flexible model can memorise; tuning stops it.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_overfit.csv ("mean" rows; ratio is the
% mean over folds of test_mae / train_mae), written by scripts/overfit_check.py
To see what the tuning protects against, we refit each model with the most
flexible setting in its own grid, the setting best able to memorise the
training windows, and compare it with the tuned model
(Table~\ref{tab:overfit}).
\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Training vs.\ held-out MAE (m) for \texttt{err\_mag\_m}, averaged over
folds, for the tuned model and its most flexible grid setting. Ratio is
held-out MAE over training MAE (mean over folds). Loose mini-batch GD: L2,
$\alpha = 10^{-4}$, $\eta = 0.01$, batch size 32.}
\label{tab:overfit}
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\hline
Model & Setting & Train MAE & Held-out MAE & Ratio \\
\hline
Ridge (L2)    & tuned ($\alpha = 1000$)              & 0.0086 & 0.0100 & 1.3 \\
              & loose ($\alpha = 0.01$)              & 0.0083 & 0.0114 & 1.5 \\
Lasso (L1)    & tuned ($\alpha = 10^{-3}$)           & 0.0089 & 0.0099 & 1.2 \\
              & loose ($\alpha = 10^{-6}$)           & 0.0083 & 0.0114 & 1.5 \\
Mini-batch GD & tuned                                & 0.0087 & 0.0100 & 1.3 \\
              & loose (see caption)                  & 0.0084 & 0.0107 & 1.4 \\
Random Forest & tuned                                & 0.0076 & 0.0102 & 1.7 \\
              & loose (unlimited depth, leaf 1)      & 0.0028 & 0.0102 & 4.0 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
With almost no penalty, Ridge and Lasso both behave like OLS: training error
drops slightly and held-out error rises to $0.0114$\,m (from $0.0100$ and
$0.0099$\,m tuned). Mini-batch GD with its weakest penalty and largest step
does the same on a smaller scale ($0.0107$ against $0.0100$\,m). (The loosest
Lasso, $\alpha = 10^{-6}$, reaches the solver's iteration limit before fully
converging; it is only used here, where it behaves like OLS as expected.) The
loose Random Forest memorises much harder (training MAE $0.0028$\,m) but its
held-out MAE does not move: averaging 300 decorrelated trees cancels much of
what the individual deep trees memorise. In every case inner cross-validation
rejected the loose setting, so the reported models are the ones with the
smallest gap.

\paragraph{The remaining gap on MH\_03 is distribution shift, not over-fitting.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m.csv (mae / train_mae, fold leave_MH_03_medium)
On the two easy folds every tuned model's held-out MAE is \emph{below} its
training MAE (ratios $0.76$--$0.87$), because those training sets include the
harder MH\_03 flight. When MH\_03 is held out, the ratio jumps to $2.1$--$3.3$
for Ridge, Lasso, mini-batch GD and Random Forest. But the mean baseline, which has no
parameters to over-fit, also jumps to $1.55$ ($0.0093 \to 0.0145$\,m). A gap
that appears even for a constant predictor comes from the test flight being
different, not from the model memorising; regularisation cannot close it.
Section~\ref{sec:failure} examines this shift.

\subsection{Failure analysis}
\label{sec:failure}

\paragraph{Gains on easy flights come mostly from the opening segment.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_failure_segments.csv
On MH\_01 and MH\_02 the first 20\,s sit at a lower drift level (mean true label
$0.1669$ and $0.1686$\,m) than the rest of the flight ($0.1883$ and $0.1891$\,m;
Figures~\ref{fig:error-over-time-mh01} and~\ref{fig:error-over-time-mh02}). The features pick this up. In that segment
Random Forest cuts the baseline's MAE from $0.0173$ to $0.0080$\,m (MH\_01) and
from $0.0193$ to $0.0104$\,m (MH\_02). After 25\,s the gain is much smaller:
$0.0086 \to 0.0078$ and $0.0083 \to 0.0073$\,m. The models track the general
drift level but not its second-to-second fluctuations.

\paragraph{MH\_03\_medium is out of distribution.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_failure_shift.csv (fold leave_MH_03_medium)
When MH\_03 is held out, the models train only on the two easy flights. MH\_03
rotates faster: its mean \texttt{gyro\_mag\_mean} is $0.295$\,rad/s against
$0.213$ in training, and $4.8\%$ of its windows fall outside the training
range. Its label is also much more spread out (std $0.0205$\,m against $0.0124$\,m
in training) and spikes to $0.301$\,m, with $5.2\%$ of its windows outside the
training label range. The models have never seen this regime.
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_failure_rotation.csv
Error also broadly grows with rotation rate: in the fastest-rotating quarter of
windows, Random Forest's MAE is $1.41\times$, $1.25\times$ and $1.63\times$ its
value in the slowest quarter (MH\_01, MH\_02, MH\_03).

\paragraph{Most of the remaining error sits in drift spikes.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_failure_spikes.csv
The worst 10\% of windows carry 28\%, 27\% and 32\% of Random Forest's total
absolute error (MH\_01, MH\_02, MH\_03). The correlation between prediction and
truth falls from $0.60$ and $0.70$ on the easy flights to $0.31$ on MH\_03. A
constant offset explains only 6\%, 10\% and 14\% of the squared error. The
failure is therefore mostly not a calibration shift that adding a constant
would fix. The models simply cannot anticipate sudden jumps in drift from
window-level summary statistics.

\paragraph{Which features matter.}
% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_importance.csv (kind = permutation):
% mean of the `importance` column over the 3 folds, x1000 to give mm.
Shuffling one feature on held-out data hurts most for the accelerometer level
features. For Random Forest these are \texttt{ax\_mean} ($+0.244$\,mm MAE) and
\texttt{ax\_rms} ($+0.159$\,mm); for Ridge they are \texttt{ax\_mean}
($+0.321$\,mm), \texttt{acc\_mag\_mean} ($+0.319$\,mm) and \texttt{ax\_rms}
($+0.293$\,mm) (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). This matches the feature
analysis of Section~\ref{sec:feature-engineering}. It also fits the label being
driven mainly by how gravity and accelerometer bias project into the
integration, not by vigorous motion as such.

\subsection{Bias-corrected target (oracle comparison)}

% Source: results/metrics/err_mag_m_bc.csv
Running the same pipeline on \texttt{err\_mag\_m\_bc}, the drift left after
subtracting the dataset's own bias estimates, the best fold-mean MAE is $0.0109$\,m
(Random Forest) against a baseline of $0.0122$\,m. $R^2$ never exceeds $0.069$
(Lasso on MH\_01) on any fold and is negative when averaged over folds for every model. Once
bias is removed, the remaining drift is barely predictable from these motion
features.

\subsection{Summary}
The classic models learn a real but modest relationship between IMU window
statistics and drift. That relationship transfers between the two easy
flights, but not to the faster MH\_03\_medium flight. The obstacles are
distribution shift and unpredictable drift spikes, not the choice of model.
Over-fitting is real but under control: unregularised OLS and the loosest
grid settings memorise the training flights and lose on new ones, and the
sequence-grouped tuning picks settings whose remaining train/test gap is
explained by the shift to MH\_03, not by memorisation.
More varied training flights (MH\_04, MH\_05) and models that see the raw
$200 \times 6$ signal are the natural next steps for Phase~2.
